  ## 3. Fields we would use
  Meanings are my reading of the response, since the docs pages I read do not

  - **severity**: Minor, Moderate, Severe, Extreme or Unknown. The sample shows

  Miami-Dade.
  - **geocode.UGC**: NWS zone or county codes, for example FLZ172.
  - **affectedZones**: URLs of those zones.

  [memory].

  ### 6. Gaps and caveats
  **Coverage and archive history**

  stated temporal domain is "Most WWA types back to 2008 or 2005, an archive of
  Flash Flood Warnings goes back to 2002 or so, and Tornado / Severe
  Thunderstorm Warnings goes back to 1986." Source:

  - **Tropical data starts too late.** In Miami and Houston the first HU record
  is in 2008 and the first TR in 2007; the first SS (storm surge) is 2017

  the archive. Houston shows no hurricane or tropical storm entry for 2005
  either.
    - Use 2008 to 2025 for the tropical group. Even then, Houston's 2008 count
  of 5 days is plausible for Ike, but I did not verify it.

  **By-point search: county and zone matches, not polygons**

  associated with the event. In the case of storm based warnings, the UGCs are
  not the official warning. Use the SBW By Point Service for the official
  warnings." [docs,
  `https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/json/vtec_events_bypoint.py?help`]
  - So the search does cover both kinds of warning, but tornado, severe
  thunderstorm and flash flood warnings are matched at county level. A small
  polygon anywhere in Cook County counts as a Chicago day [observed: ugc
  `ILC031`].
  - I did not test the SBW By Point service. It is listed on the `/api/` page
  and would give the official polygons.

  **Duplicates and event identity**
  - One city point returns both a county code and a forecast-zone code. The
  returned codes were Minneapolis `MNC053` and `MNZ060`, Chicago `ILC031`,
  `ILZ014` and `ILZ104`, Dallas `TXC113` and `TXZ119`, and Miami `FLC086`,
  `FLZ074` and `FLZ173` [observed].
  - That means one real-world event can appear as 2 to 3 records. Winter and
  cold products, which are zone-based, show up under zone codes.
  - A distinct event is `(wfo, year, phenomena, significance, eventid)`. By that
  key, Minneapolis had 773 records and 767 events, Chicago 1,544 and 1,534, and
  Miami 3,196 and 3,163 [observed].
  - IEM warns the id is not always unique: "A tracking number that should be
  unique for this event, but sometimes it is not" [docs, same vtec.html page].
  - I saw this directly in Miami in 2005. The same key, `(MFL, 2005, SV, W, 1)`,
  appeared with unrelated issue times 3 months apart [observed]. Events with
  the same key but non-overlapping times are therefore separate events.
  - IEM also says each event shows only its last known state: "provides the
  metadata for the last known state of the event" [docs]. The `issue` and
  `expire` values are the final ones after extensions, which is what a day count
  needs.
  - My day counts are a set union of dates, so duplicates and zone overlap do
  not inflate them. A count of records or events would be inflated, so use days,
  not counts.
  - Time zones: all times are UTC [docs]. A winter storm crossing midnight local
  time can add a day compared with local-day counting.

  **Other limits**

  and EC renaming in 2025. Zone boundaries can change over time. Neither was
  tested.
  - A warning is a forecast alert, not proof that disruption happened.
  - Very long river-flood (FL) warnings are not a problem here, but they would

  ## 8. Gaps and caveats
  - Granularity is county-level, with no site-level data. The layer is a polygon
  service you can also query by geometry, but values are county aggregates.
  - Scores and ratings mix hazard with how much property and population a county
  has. Cook Cold Wave has RISKS 100 but `ALR_NPCTL` 4.9 and building loss rate
  2.8e-9, because its $700M EALT is mostly population dollars valued at $13.7M
  per life. For comparing hazard intensity, use the building-based `ALRB` /
  `ALR_NPCTL`. For dollars at stake, use `EALT`. `RISKS` also folds in social
  vulnerability and community resilience.
  - `RISKS` is a percentile among about 3,100 counties, so it saturates near 100
  for large counties. Ratings are k-means clusters, not fixed cut-offs.
  `ALR_NPCTL` is relative only within counties where the hazard applies.
  - AFREQ units mix events and event-days, so compare across hazards with care.
  The Hail and Strong Wind AFREQ values are annualized counts over a 49-km grid
  (TD Table 5) and can exceed 1 per year; I did not check how the counts are
  aggregated.
  - Vintages differ: Ice Storm data ends 2014, Hail, Strong Wind, Tornado and
  Inland Flooding end 2023, Cold Wave and Winter Weather cover only 19 years,
  and Hurricane goes back to 1851. The TD says EAL "represent[s] an

  the long-record hazards you wanted. The Coastal Flooding frequency is modeled
  (floodplain constants of 0.01 and 0.002 plus High Tide Flooding), not
  observed.
  - Service names such as `NRI_Counties_v117` and `NRI_Counties_Prod_v1181_view`
